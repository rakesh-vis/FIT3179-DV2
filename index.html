Health Isn't Evenly Shared

Where you live, how much you earn and who you are all shape your chances of being healthy in Australia.

Rakesh Viswanathan · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · October 2026

Australia is known for its love of sport and active, outdoor lifestyles. Yet around 3 in 4 Australians aged 15 and over don’t meet the physical activity guidelines. Inactivity is just one of several health risks, alongside obesity, smoking, psychological distress and food insecurity, and these risks aren’t spread evenly. This page explores how where you live, how much you have and who you are shape your health. It aims to help Australians see who carries these risks and where support is needed most.

Part 1

Where you live

Obesity is highest in regional areas, especially in eastern Australia and Tasmania. Rates range from about 16% of adults in the City of Adelaide to almost half (49.5%) in Tasmania’s West Coast. Grey areas have no estimate; most are remote areas with small populations. Drag the slider below the map to fade out areas under a chosen rate. Try 40% to see where obesity is highest.

Source: PHIDU Social Health Atlas of Australia (Local Government Areas), modelled estimates for 2022. Boundaries: ABS ASGS Edition 3, LGA 2021.

Circle size shows how many people aren’t meeting the physical activity guidelines, while colour shows the rate. The biggest circles are in the cities: Brisbane alone has about 750,000 inactive people, yet its rate (73%) is below average. The darkest circles are in regional towns such as Junee (NSW) and Whyalla (SA), where close to 9 in 10 people miss the guidelines.

Use the "Show" menu under the map to zoom in on a city or region.

Source: PHIDU Social Health Atlas of Australia (Local Government Areas), 2022. Circles placed at LGA centroids. Boundaries: ABS LGA 2021.

Cities vs the regions

Each line represents a risk factor: the steeper the line, the bigger the gap between the cities and the regions. All five risks are higher outside the capital cities. Obesity has the largest gap, at 29.4% in the capital cities compared with 36.5% in the rest of the state. Risky drinking is close behind (17.7% vs 24.1%), while smoking shows the biggest relative jump, from 10.9% to 15.1%, about 40% higher.

Source: PHIDU Social Health Atlas of Australia, 2022 (Greater Capital City Statistical Areas vs Rest of State/NT, age-standardised).

A closer look at Melbourne

Zooming in on Melbourne shows where people with high psychological distress live within one city. Each dot represents 200 adults with high or very high psychological distress, placed randomly inside their council; in total, there are about 474,000. Casey has the most dots because of its large population. Frankston has the highest rate (15.6% of adults), while Bayside, further up the bay, has a rate of just 8.8%, almost half.

Source: PHIDU Social Health Atlas of Australia (Local Government Areas), 2022. 1 dot = 200 people, placed randomly within each council. Boundaries: ABS LGA 2021.

Part 2

How much you have

Council areas are ranked by the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ measure of socio-economic disadvantage, which looks at things like income, education and employment, and then split into five equal groups. Each dot represents a council area, and the coral line marks the middle value for each group. As areas become more disadvantaged, the line moves towards worse health: smoking is more than twice as common in the most disadvantaged areas (17.4%) as in the least (8.0%), and the same pattern holds for every measure.

Use the "Show" menu under the chart to switch between health measures.

Source: PHIDU Social Health Atlas of Australia (Local Government Areas), 2022. Disadvantage: ABS Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (IRSD), 2021.

Part 3

Food on the table

Source: ABS, Food Security, 2023 (National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey).

How many go without?

About 1 in 8 Australian households (13%, or roughly 1.3 million) don’t always have enough to eat. Each square represents 1% of households, and the darker the coral, the more severe the situation. Food insecurity ranges from marginal (worrying about running out of food), to moderate (eating less variety or lower-quality food), to severe (skipping meals or going without food), which affects 3.4% of households.

Disadvantage and food

Disadvantage shapes not just health, but whether families have enough to eat. In the most disadvantaged 20% of areas, food insecurity is more common at every level, and the gap widens as it gets more serious. Severe food insecurity affects 7.8% of households in these areas, compared with just 1.5% in the least disadvantaged areas: five times higher.

Source: ABS, Food Security, 2023. Areas grouped by ABS IRSD quintile.

Source: ABS, Food Security, 2023. Income = equivalised gross weekly household income.

Income matters

Income tells a similar story. Households are split into five equal groups by income, adjusted for household size. Food insecurity falls steadily as income rises: almost 1 in 4 households (23.2%) in the lowest-income group are food insecure, compared with just 3.6% in the highest, about six times lower.

Who is in the household

Who lives in a household also matters. One in three lone-parent families (34%) is food insecure, three times the rate of couple families with children (11.3%). Group households, such as share houses, follow at 27.8%, while families without children are the least affected (8.4%).

Source: ABS, Food Security, 2023.

Source: ABS, Food Security, 2023.

How severe is it?

Breaking food insecurity down by severity reveals a different picture. Although lone-parent families have the highest overall rate, group households have the most severe food insecurity: 14.1% skip meals or go without food, more than half of all food-insecure group households. For couple families and families without children, food insecurity is mostly marginal or moderate, with less than 2% going without.

Part 4

Who you are

Health risks also differ between men and women. Men are more than twice as likely to drink at risky levels (27.5% compared with 12.1%) and are more likely to smoke, vape and be overweight. Obesity is similar for both, but women report more high psychological distress (16.1% compared with 11.2%), the only risk where women are clearly higher.

Source: ABS, National Health Survey, 2022 (Tables 7, 8, 12 and 14). *Risky drinking and vaping are for people aged 15+.

What it adds up to

Health risks aren’t spread evenly across Australia. Where you live, how much you have and who you are all shape your chances of being healthy. Risks are higher outside the capital cities, and more disadvantaged areas experience worse health. Low-income households and lone-parent families are hit hardest by food insecurity, and the gaps grow as it becomes more severe. Men take more physical risks, while women report more distress. The common finding is that disadvantage tends to stack up: the same areas and households often face several risks at once. Being aware of these patterns is the first step, and knowing where support should go can help steer Australia in the right direction.
